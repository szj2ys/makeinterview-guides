How to answer “What would you do differently?”

Show learning that changes a future decision without rewriting the past or blaming others.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Name one decision you controlled and the evidence you had at the time.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“What would you do differently on that project?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Identify the signal, assumption, or process gap that would have changed the decision.
  4. Make the decision visible. Describe the specific alternative and its own tradeoff.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Explain how the learning has already changed another project and how you would test whether the change works.
Worked rehearsal

“I would have tested the import with the largest realistic file before committing the release date. I had time to see the file-size distribution, but I treated the sample as representative without checking it. A longer test would have delayed coding by one day, which was a worthwhile trade for avoiding a missed customer deadline. On the next migration I added a size-profile check during planning. It changed the batch design before implementation, so the new process has one clear test of usefulness.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What would you keep from the original decision?
  • How do you know the new behavior is better?
  • Which alternative also had a cost?

Common failure to avoid

Avoid a vague answer such as “communicate more.” Name the decision, alternative, tradeoff, and later evidence.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The changed decision is in your control.
  • The missed evidence is specific.
  • The alternative has a stated cost.
  • The lesson has been applied elsewhere.
  • The improvement can be observed or falsified.
