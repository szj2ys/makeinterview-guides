How to answer a metrics or business-impact question

Explain a metric’s definition, baseline, confounder, and the decision it informed.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Name the user behavior or business outcome you intended to change.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“How did you measure the impact of your work?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Define the numerator, denominator, time window, and baseline before describing the intervention.
  4. Make the decision visible. Explain what else could have changed the result and how you treated that uncertainty.
  5. Land the result, then stop. State the observed result and what it did or did not justify changing.
Worked rehearsal

“For a setup redesign, the target was successful completion within ten minutes, not raw clicks. The baseline was 62 percent over four weeks. We launched to existing users sequentially, but a marketing campaign started in the same period, so I compared new cohorts against the control and checked support volume. Completion rose eight points in the treatment cohort, with no comparable move in the control. Because the segment was small, we expanded the change in phases rather than claiming a permanent company-wide effect.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • Why was that metric the right proxy?
  • What confounder worried you most?
  • What result would have made you stop?

Common failure to avoid

Do not present a metric without its definition or baseline. A number with the wrong denominator is not evidence.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The metric has numerator and denominator.
  • The baseline and time window are stated.
  • The attribution limit is acknowledged.
  • The result includes comparison or context.
  • The next decision fits the strength of the evidence.
