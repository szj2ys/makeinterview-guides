How to answer “Why are you changing careers?”

Show transferable evidence while respecting the new field’s distinct standards.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Name the target work and the specific limitation of your previous track.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“Why are you changing careers?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Describe one bridge experience that demonstrates relevant judgment.
  4. Make the decision visible. Explain what training, project, or feedback convinced you to make the change.
  5. Land the result, then stop. State the gap you are still closing and how you will close it with support.
Worked rehearsal

“I’m moving from customer operations into product management because I keep being pulled toward defining the problem rather than only resolving the case. I built a small internal intake redesign with the product team, but I lacked ownership of tradeoffs and measurement. I completed a part-time product course and ran three advisory interviews before deciding to apply. I still need more experience setting long-term priorities; I would close that by owning one bounded area and reviewing decisions with a manager.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What would make you go back to the previous field?
  • Which part of the new discipline is hardest for you?
  • What feedback have practitioners given you?

Common failure to avoid

Do not claim that former expertise automatically makes you senior in the new field. Name the transfer and the gap together.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The new direction is concrete.
  • The bridge evidence is real.
  • A training or feedback loop exists.
  • The remaining gap is acknowledged.
  • The answer distinguishes interest from demonstrated work.
