How to answer “What is your greatest weakness?”

Describe a real working limitation, the mechanism you use to reduce it, and evidence that the mechanism is active.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Choose a limitation that does not remove a core requirement of the role.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“What is your greatest weakness?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Describe a moment when the limitation showed up and its practical cost.
  4. Make the decision visible. Explain the specific habit or system you use now.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Show the change without claiming perfection; give the interviewer a reason to test the claim.
Worked rehearsal

“I can over-invest in polishing a draft before sharing it. On a research summary, I spent an extra day editing structure before the stakeholders had seen the findings. I now send a one-page evidence memo with the first useful version and reserve detailed editing for decisions that need it. The improvement is that reviews happen earlier, but I still need deadlines that force a stop. I would rather be tested on that habit than claim I’ve fixed it completely.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What feedback led you to notice it?
  • Who checks whether the habit is improving?
  • When was the last time it caused a problem?

Common failure to avoid

Avoid disguised strengths such as perfectionism, and avoid weaknesses that would make the role unsafe or impossible.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The limitation has real cost.
  • The response includes an operating mechanism.
  • There is evidence after the change.
  • The weakness does not contradict a must-have requirement.
  • The ending invites verification instead of claiming perfection.
