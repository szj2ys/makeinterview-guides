How to answer a conflict-with-a-teammate question

Show how you separated interpersonal friction from the decision the work needed.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

State the disagreement in operational terms rather than assigning motives.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“Tell me about a conflict with a teammate.”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Give the facts each side could observe and the moment the cost became clear.
  4. Make the decision visible. Explain how you changed the conversation, including what you conceded.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Describe the decision, the relationship or process after it, and what you would handle differently.
Worked rehearsal

“A designer and I disagreed about whether to ship a simplified form without a review. I wanted the review because customer support had documented several ambiguous cases; the designer was protecting a launch date. We wrote the disputed cases on one page and agreed to test the form with three support agents. Two failures appeared, so we kept the review but cut two optional fields. The launch moved only by one day. I did not start calmly, so next time I would put the evidence on the page before making the deadline case.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What did the other person think you missed?
  • What would you do if they still disagreed?
  • How did you repair the working relationship?

Common failure to avoid

Do not describe a story where you were right and everyone else was unreasonable. The review is testing perspective-taking and process.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • Both positions are represented fairly.
  • The answer names the cost of continuing to disagree.
  • You concede at least one point.
  • The result includes both task and relationship evidence.
  • The reflection changes a future action.
