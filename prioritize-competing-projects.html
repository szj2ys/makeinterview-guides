How to answer a prioritization question

Explain a decision method tied to impact, urgency, risk, and the cost of delay.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Name the decision owner and the constraint you use to compare work.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“How do you prioritize competing projects?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Show how you distinguish a loud request from a consequential one.
  4. Make the decision visible. Describe what you explicitly defer and how the owner understands the tradeoff.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Provide an example where the reordering changed an outcome and a review later confirmed the choice.
Worked rehearsal

“I prioritize against the outcome that is most expensive to delay, then check whether the work also reduces irreversible risk. On a release week, sales wanted a demo request and support brought a payment ambiguity. I asked both owners for the consequence of waiting one week. The demo could move; the payment issue could produce incorrect charges, so it went first. We delayed the demo by two days and fixed the billing edge case before any invoices ran. I recorded the decision so the next prioritization call did not reopen the same argument.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What happens when two requests have the same impact?
  • Who can overrule your priority?
  • How do you handle work that is urgent but strategically irrelevant?

Common failure to avoid

Avoid listing frameworks without a real example. Memorized acronyms do not show how tradeoffs were negotiated.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The method names the decision criteria.
  • At least one item is explicitly deferred.
  • The affected owner knows the consequence.
  • The example contains an outcome.
  • The decision record supports later review.
