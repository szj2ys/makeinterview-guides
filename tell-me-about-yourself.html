How to answer “Tell me about yourself”

Give a two-minute professional introduction that connects your past work to the role in front of you.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Start with the role you are targeting, then name the thread that connects your last two or three experiences.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“Tell me about yourself.”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Pick one accomplishment that is relevant to the job description and one piece of scope that explains why you were trusted with it.
  4. Make the decision visible. Show the choice that moved your career toward this work rather than narrating your résumé in chronological order.
  5. Land the result, then stop. End with the result you produced and why that result makes this role the logical next step.
Worked rehearsal

“I’m a B2B product manager focused on making complex workflows usable by operations teams. In my last role, customers were abandoning a setup flow because responsibility was split across three teams. I mapped the handoffs, got engineering and support to agree on one owner per step, and reduced support questions in the first week. I’m here because this role puts that same cross-functional problem at the center of the product.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • Which part of that result was yours versus the team’s?
  • Why is this role the next step rather than a lateral move?
  • What in our job description attracted you most, and what evidence backs that up?

Common failure to avoid

Do not recite every employer or begin with a long personal history. The interviewer is testing relevance and judgment, not completeness.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The target role appears in the first two sentences.
  • One concrete result supports the narrative.
  • Your personal contribution is distinguishable from the team’s.
  • The ending explains fit without claiming you know the company better than you do.
  • The answer can be delivered in about 90–120 seconds.
