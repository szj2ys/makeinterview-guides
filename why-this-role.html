How to answer “Why this role?”

Connect a specific part of the job to a demonstrated strength and a deliberate next step.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Name the work you want more of and the work you want less of; both make the choice credible.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“Why this role?”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Use one past project to show that the role’s hardest requirement is already familiar to you.
  4. Make the decision visible. Explain the constraint you are seeking, such as customer proximity, broader ownership, or a different problem scale.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Close with what you expect to contribute in the first few months and how you would judge whether it is working.
Worked rehearsal

“I want to own the full discovery-to-launch loop for a customer-facing workflow. In my current role I can improve a feature after sales has specified it, but I do not get to test the underlying problem directly. Last quarter I joined six customer calls outside my remit and rewrote the acceptance criteria around the actual handoff failure. The launch reduced rework. This role makes that customer evidence loop the job rather than a side project.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • Which responsibility are you least prepared for?
  • What would make you leave this role?
  • How would you know after 90 days that you chose correctly?

Common failure to avoid

Do not answer with praise for the company’s mission alone. A mission statement cannot show why the daily work fits you.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The answer names a concrete part of the role.
  • A past example proves the claimed strength.
  • The motivation includes a real tradeoff.
  • The first-90-day contribution is plausible and bounded.
  • No statement depends on information you have not researched.
