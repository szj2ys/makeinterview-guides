How to answer a project-failure question

Choose a real failure with a controllable decision you now handle differently.

What the interviewer is listening for

The interviewer is not looking for a polished speech. They are checking whether you can choose the right evidence, explain a decision you controlled, and keep the facts consistent when the next question makes the situation less comfortable.

Say in one sentence what outcome failed and what consequence followed.

Build the answer in five moves

  1. Set the question. Restate the real prompt—“Tell me about a project that failed.”—in your own words and name the decision you need to explain.
  2. Use one example. Select a situation where you can distinguish your contribution from the team’s and where the outcome can be checked.
  3. Show the evidence. Describe the earliest signal you missed or the assumption you did not test.
  4. Make the decision visible. Show the escalation, containment, or recovery action you took once the problem was visible.
  5. Land the result, then stop. Separate the outcome you could not recover from the learning you retained.
Worked rehearsal

“A migration missed its customer deadline because we estimated row processing from a sample that did not include the largest accounts. I had approved the estimate without asking how the sample was selected. When the gap appeared, I stopped the cutover, told support and affected customers the same day, and moved the largest accounts to a separate batch. We finished nine days late. Now I require the sampling method and worst-case shape in every migration review; that check has changed two later plans.”

Follow-up drill

After saying the answer once without notes, ask these questions out loud. The goal is to add evidence without inventing a new story:

  • What did you personally decide that contributed to the failure?
  • Which stakeholder received the bad news, and how?
  • What would success have looked like earlier?

Common failure to avoid

Do not choose a failure caused entirely by another team. The interviewer needs to see your decision point.

If the interviewer challenges a fact, separate what you owned from what the team owned. Correct an overstated claim immediately. A smaller, accurate result survives follow-up questions better than a larger result that cannot be explained.

Self-check before you record

  • The failure and consequence are explicit.
  • The root cause names a decision you controlled.
  • Containment happened before blame.
  • The outcome is not rewritten as a success.
  • The follow-up habit has later evidence.
